# Article Drafting Guideline

## Purpose

Use this guideline to prepare a Design subsite article from design experience. The preliminary questions are intended to capture enough of the real story for a coherent working draft without inventing important facts.

Design subsite theme: **Reasoning Processes**

The article should explain how design thinking was used to understand and solve a particular problem. The final artifact matters, but the central story is the reasoning, decisions, constraints, alternatives, and discoveries that shaped it.

---

## Part 1: Preliminary Questions

Answer these questions, tell the design problem and the thinking that shaped the direction rather than trying to document a formal design process :

1. **What was the situation that needed to be designed?**  
   Describe the context, the people or organization involved, the problem you noticed, and what made the situation difficult or interesting to solve.

2. **What did you decide the design needed to do?**  
   Describe the direction you took, the decisions that shaped it, and the reasoning behind those decisions. Include research, observations, references, alternatives, or constraints when they actually influenced the direction.

3. **What made you change or confirm your direction?**  
   Describe the moments that altered your thinking, whether through discovery, feedback, experimentation, constraints, or simply seeing the design take shape.

4. **What did the design make you understand differently?**  
   Reflect on what the project taught you about the original problem, your design approach, or the relationship between the two. Include what you would reconsider today.

---

## Part 2: Editorial Drafting

Use the completed preliminary answers as the foundational source to assemble the working draft following this editorial instruction :

```md
Draft a working article for the **Design** subsite, whose theme is **Reasoning Processes**.

The source material below contains my actual design project story and reasoning. Treat it as factual source material. Do not invent research, decisions, motivations, feedback, outcomes, or design details that are not supported by it. If something is unclear, preserve the uncertainty rather than filling the gap with assumptions.

The article should be about how I reasoned through a design problem, not simply a showcase of the final artifact. The reader should understand where the problem came from, what I initially thought, what I needed to learn, how the problem changed through investigation, why I made the important design decisions, and what I learned from the result.

Preserve the internal reasoning and personal perspective. Do not flatten the story into a generic UX/design case study. When the source material contains a moment of uncertainty, disagreement, realization, constraint, or reconsideration, use it to put the reader closer to the thinking that actually happened.

Build a clear article structure with useful section headings. Avoid a process-template structure such as "Research", "Ideation", "Design", and "Conclusion" unless the story genuinely requires those sections. The structure should follow the strongest reasoning arc of this particular project.

Treat the final design as evidence of the reasoning rather than the only subject of the article. Do not spend space describing visual details that do not contribute to understanding why the design became what it was.

Preserve limitations and unresolved questions when they are part of the real story. Do not manufacture a neat success story from an imperfect or unfinished project.

Writing constraints :

- Use first person where appropriate.
- Preserve my personal reasoning and voice.
- Every paragraph must contain multiple sentences and develop a complete thought. Never use a single-sentence paragraph.
- Keep the article accessible to a general audience while retaining precise design terminology where useful.
- Avoid unnecessary rhetorical questions. Use one only when it naturally emerges from the story.
- Do not use em dashes.
- Do not use inline colons or semicolons.
- Avoid repetitive section names and repeated phrasing such as "Beyond the..." throughout the article.
- Avoid generic UX case-study language and empty statements about "the importance of design".
- Target approximately 650–1000 words unless the source material clearly requires a different length.
- Do not add a "Conclusion" section unless explicitly requested.

After drafting, check the article for factual fidelity, repeated ideas, weak transitions, unnecessary artifact description, and places where the internal reasoning has been replaced by generic design language.

```
